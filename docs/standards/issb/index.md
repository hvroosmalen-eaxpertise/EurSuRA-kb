# ISSB — International Sustainability Standards Board

The International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB) is the global standard-setter for sustainability-related financial disclosure. It was established by the [[IFRS Foundation]] at COP26 in Glasgow (November 2021) and sits alongside the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB) within the Foundation's two-board structure. Its mandate is to develop a global baseline of sustainability disclosure standards that meet the information needs of investors and other providers of capital. The resulting standards are issued as *IFRS Sustainability Disclosure Standards*; the first two — IFRS S1 and IFRS S2 — were released in June 2023 and apply for annual reporting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2024.

The ISSB consolidated several earlier initiatives: the [[Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD)]], whose four-pillar architecture is embedded in both IFRS S1 and IFRS S2 and whose monitoring responsibilities moved to the IFRS Foundation on 1 January 2024; the [[Sustainability Accounting Standards Board]] (SASB) Standards, now hosted by the Foundation as industry-specific application material; the Climate Disclosure Standards Board (CDSB); and the Integrated Reporting Framework.

The ISSB sits under *Standards* in this KB, not *Frameworks*: IFRS S1 and IFRS S2 are formal disclosure standards, issued by a recognised standard-setter and designed to be endorsed into national or regional reporting regimes, not voluntary reporting frameworks.

## IFRS S1 — General Requirements for Disclosure of Sustainability-related Financial Information

IFRS S1 is the "umbrella" standard. It sets out the core content requirements an entity must apply to every sustainability-related risk and opportunity, and the conceptual foundations — materiality, reporting entity, connectivity with the financial statements, and sources of guidance — that run through all ISSB standards.

**Objective.** IFRS S1 requires disclosure of information about sustainability-related risks and opportunities that could reasonably be expected to affect the entity's cash flows, access to finance, or cost of capital over the short, medium or long term — the ISSB's single-materiality lens, often described as *enterprise value* or *financial* materiality. This is a narrower lens than the [[Double Materiality]] used by the [[European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS)]], which adds impact materiality.

**Core content pillars.** For every material risk and opportunity, an entity reports along the four TCFD-derived pillars:

1. **Governance** — the governance processes, controls and procedures the entity uses to monitor, manage and oversee sustainability-related risks and opportunities.
2. **Strategy** — the entity's strategy for managing those risks and opportunities, including resilience of its strategy and business model under different climate-related and other scenarios.
3. **Risk management** — the processes to identify, assess, prioritise and monitor the risks and opportunities.
4. **Metrics and targets** — the metrics used to measure performance, including progress against targets set by the entity or by law or regulation.

**Sources of guidance.** Where a specific IFRS Sustainability Disclosure Standard does not address a particular sustainability-related risk or opportunity, an entity must consider the SASB Standards, CDSB Framework application guidance, the most recent pronouncements of other standard-setting bodies whose requirements are designed to meet investor information needs, and the sustainability-related risks and opportunities identified by entities in the same industries or geographies.

**Connectivity.** The information in the sustainability disclosures must be published at the same time as the related financial statements and cover the same reporting entity and reporting period. Entities must explain, where applicable, how data flows between the sustainability disclosures and the financial statements (for example, assumptions used in both).

## IFRS S2 — Climate-related Disclosures

IFRS S2 is the first topic-specific ISSB standard. It applies to climate-related risks (physical and transition) and climate-related opportunities, and it uses the IFRS S1 four-pillar structure. Where IFRS S1 says *what* to disclose for any topic, IFRS S2 fills in the climate-specific requirements.

**Metrics and targets** include:

- Scope 1, [[Scope 2]] and [[Scope 3]] greenhouse-gas emissions, measured in accordance with the [[Greenhouse Gas Protocol (GHG Protocol)]] (unless the entity is required by a jurisdictional authority or exchange to use a different methodology);
- the amount and percentage of assets or business activities vulnerable to transition risks, to physical risks, and aligned with climate-related opportunities;
- capital expenditure, financing or investment deployed toward climate-related risks and opportunities;
- internal carbon prices;
- the extent to which executive remuneration is tied to climate-related performance; and
- the entity's climate-related targets and progress toward them.

**Scenario analysis.** Entities must use climate-related scenario analysis to assess the resilience of their strategy and business model. The depth of analysis is scaled to the circumstances of the entity, including its skills and resources.

**Industry-based disclosure topics.** Appendix B of IFRS S2 incorporates the industry-based disclosure topics drawn from the SASB Standards, giving IFRS S2 a sector overlay without a separate standard per industry.

## Relationship to TCFD

IFRS S2 is the TCFD recommendations re-expressed as a formal standard. The four pillars, the eleven recommended disclosures, the strategy / resilience language, and the metrics-and-targets reporting all survive — hardened, in several places sharpened (for example, mandatory scope 1–3 disclosure, mandatory scenario analysis). The TCFD was formally wound down in 2023, and the IFRS Foundation now monitors company progress on climate disclosures previously reported against TCFD.

## EU Interoperability — ESRS and ISSB

The European Commission and EFRAG, together with the ISSB, published interoperability guidance to minimise reporting duplication between ESRS and the ISSB standards. The two sets of standards share a common climate-metrics vocabulary and are both anchored in the GHG Protocol and the TCFD architecture. A company that discloses under ESRS can produce an IFRS S1 / S2-compliant statement with modest additional disclosure, principally around financial-materiality framing, scenario analysis specifics, and industry-based SASB topics. The main structural difference remains the materiality lens: ESRS requires double materiality, while ISSB applies single (financial / enterprise-value) materiality.

## Jurisdictional Uptake

ISSB standards are adopted into national regulation through local endorsement. By late 2026, more than twenty jurisdictions — including the United Kingdom (via UK SRS, forthcoming), Canada (CSSB), Japan (SSBJ), Australia, Singapore, Hong Kong, Brazil, Nigeria and others — have confirmed roadmaps for adoption or endorsement, in each case with phased application for large listed entities first. In the EU, ISSB disclosures are not required in addition to ESRS; the two regimes are maintained in parallel with the shared interoperability guidance.

## Relevance for EU Companies and SMEs

For EU companies in the scope of CSRD, ESRS reporting generally subsumes what ISSB would require. For companies with global capital-markets exposure — listings or financing outside the EU — IFRS S1 / S2 are the baseline the market increasingly asks for, and the ESRS-ISSB interoperability work means the incremental burden is limited. [[SMEs]] in EU supply chains should expect requests for GHG emissions and basic climate-governance data phrased in ISSB vocabulary from large multinational buyers that report under IFRS S1 / S2.
