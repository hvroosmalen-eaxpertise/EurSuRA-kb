# Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive

The Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (CSDDD), formally Directive (EU) 2024/1760, is the European Union instrument that turns risk-based human-rights and environmental due diligence into a binding obligation for in-scope companies. It was adopted on 13 June 2024 and entered into force on 25 July 2024; Member States must transpose it by 26 July 2026. The directive complements the disclosure obligations of the [[Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD)]] by regulating conduct rather than reporting: where CSRD asks what a company says about its impacts, CSDDD asks what it does about them.

CSDDD is anchored in international soft-law instruments — the [[UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights]] and the [[OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises]] — and translates their expectation of ongoing [[Due Diligence in Responsible Business Conduct]] into Union law. Non-compliance carries both administrative sanctions and civil liability for damages suffered because of a failure to prevent an impact a company could have prevented.

## Scope and Phased Application

The directive applies to EU companies above size thresholds and to non-EU companies with significant Union turnover. Following the 2025 *Omnibus* simplification package, the thresholds were raised and the roll-out slowed. The current phasing is:

| Phase | Applicable from | EU companies | Non-EU companies (Union turnover) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 26 July 2027 | > 5 000 employees and > €1.5 bn turnover | > €1.5 bn |
| 2 | 26 July 2028 | > 3 000 employees and > €900 m turnover | > €900 m |
| 3 | 26 July 2029 | > 1 000 employees and > €450 m turnover | > €450 m |

[[SMEs]] are not directly in scope. They are, however, exposed indirectly through contractual due-diligence cascades from in-scope buyers — the same supply-chain propagation seen with the CSRD — and the directive explicitly instructs in-scope companies to limit the burden they place on SME counterparties, including through targeted support.

## The Six-Step Due Diligence Process

CSDDD codifies the OECD / UNGP risk-based model and extends it across a company's *chain of activities*: its own operations, its subsidiaries, and the operations of its direct and indirect business partners upstream (and parts of downstream activities such as distribution and transport, excluding end-use and disposal).

The six due-diligence steps that in-scope companies must integrate into policy and management systems are:

1. **Integrate due diligence into policies and risk-management systems.** A standing due-diligence policy, developed with employees and their representatives, and reviewed at least every 24 months.
2. **Identify and assess actual and potential adverse impacts.** Risk-based mapping of impacts on human rights and the environment across own operations and the chain of activities.
3. **Prevent and mitigate potential impacts; bring actual impacts to an end or minimise them.** Includes action plans, contractual assurances cascading down the chain, investments, and, where needed, suspension or termination of business relationships as a last resort.
4. **Provide remediation** for actual adverse impacts the company caused or jointly caused — remediation proportional to involvement.
5. **Carry out meaningful [[stakeholder engagement]].** Affected [[workers in the value chain]], [[affected communities]], and their legitimate representatives must be consulted at identification, prevention, remediation and monitoring stages.
6. **Monitor, verify and communicate.** Periodic assessment of the effectiveness of measures and public reporting — in practice through the company's CSRD sustainability statement where applicable.

In-scope companies must also operate a notification mechanism and a complaints procedure, open to affected persons and their representatives, trade unions, and civil-society organisations.

## Climate Transition Plan

Article 22 requires every in-scope company to adopt and put into effect a climate transition plan aligned with the 1.5 °C goal of the [[Paris Agreement]] and the EU's 2050 climate-neutrality objective, consistent with the interim target set in the European Climate Law. Where a company already discloses a transition plan under ESRS E1 of the [[European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS)]], that disclosure is deemed to satisfy Article 22 — the two regimes deliberately converge on a single plan rather than two parallel documents.

## Enforcement, Penalties and Civil Liability

Each Member State designates a supervisory authority. Supervisors may require remedial action, impose administrative penalties — pecuniary penalties capped at not less than 5 % of a company's net worldwide turnover — and publicly name non-compliant companies. The European Commission coordinates a European Network of Supervisory Authorities.

CSDDD also establishes a harmonised civil-liability regime: a company is liable for damages caused by its intentional or negligent failure to prevent or bring to an end an adverse impact it should have addressed. Trade unions and non-governmental human-rights or environmental organisations may represent affected persons in such actions. Member States must ensure that this liability is of overriding mandatory application in private-international-law terms, so that it cannot be contracted away by a choice of non-EU law.

## Relationship to CSRD, ESRS and the Omnibus Package

The CSRD, CSDDD and the EU Taxonomy together form the EU's sustainable-finance *acquis*: CSRD requires disclosure, CSDDD regulates conduct, and the [[EU Taxonomy]] defines what counts as a sustainable activity. The February 2025 *Omnibus I* proposals narrowed CSRD scope, deferred CSDDD obligations by a year, raised CSDDD thresholds, limited the depth of value-chain due diligence (focusing it on tier-1 partners by default), removed the harmonised civil-liability regime at EU level in favour of national rules, and reduced the maximum frequency of policy review from annual to every 24 months. The simplification package was adopted during 2025 and is reflected in the figures and dates above.

## Implications for SMEs

An SME outside the direct scope of CSDDD will still feel it through contractual flow-down: in-scope buyers will ask for supplier codes of conduct, risk disclosures, audit rights and, in some cases, remediation commitments. The directive expects buyers to use proportionate requests and to offer support — training, financial contribution, help with the development of a corrective action plan — rather than simply pushing the burden downward. For an SME that already reports under [[Voluntary Sustainability Reporting Standard for SMEs (VSME)]], the VSME Comprehensive module's workforce, value-chain-worker and affected-community datapoints give most of what a CSDDD-obligated buyer will ask for.
