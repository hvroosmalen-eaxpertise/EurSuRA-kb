--- # The State of Sustainability in Higher Education 2015: Emissions Metrics, Consumption Trends & Strategies for Success The State of Sustainability in Higher Education 2015 report provides an analysis of emissions metrics, consumption trends, and strategies for success across U.S. higher education institutions. It evaluates the effectiveness of sustainability initiatives, including energy conservation, efficiency improvements, and fuel-switching efforts, and explores how external factors such as public policy and energy costs influence these outcomes. The report also highlights the importance of standardized data collection and institutional commitment in advancing sustainability goals. ## Background In 2008, Second Nature published a guidance document for signatories to the American College & University Presidentsâ€™ Climate Commitment, now known as the Carbon Commitment. This document introduced the "carbon management hierarchy," a framework representing best practices for managing energy and greenhouse gas emissions both within and beyond higher education. Over the following decade, U.S. campuses invested significant resources into addressing the challenges outlined in this hierarchy, including conservation, efficiency, fuel-switching, and offsetting. This report presents the first comprehensive assessment of emissions and energy trends from a nationally representative set of institutions, examining the effectiveness of these initiatives and the factors influencing their success. ## Campus Carbon Footprints in Context: How We Got Here In 2002, a small number of colleges and universities began measuring and publicly reporting their carbon footprints, largely to demonstrate the potential implications of meeting targets established by the Kyoto Protocol to the 1992 United Nations Framework on Climate Change. Most campuses used the Campus Carbon Calculatorâ„¢, an Excel-based tool developed in partnership between the University of New Hampshire and the nonprofit Clean Air-Cool Planet (CA-CP). At that time, sustainability was not yet a mainstream focus in higher education, with very few institutions having dedicated sustainability departments. Over the next five years, the field of campus sustainability grew rapidly, with much of the momentum centered on climate and energy issues. The launch of the Carbon Commitment in 2007 epitomized this focus, marking a significant shift in institutional commitment to sustainability. ## Campus Carbon Reporting Frameworks: A Primer By the time the Carbon Commitment was launched, hundreds of schools had already begun voluntarily measuring and publicly reporting their carbon footprints. However, data sharing remained inconsistent, with institutions disseminating information through hundreds of diverse publications and websites. Most of these institutions continued using the Campus Carbon Calculatorâ„¢ (CCC), which had been continually developed in alignment with global carbon accounting standards known as the Greenhouse Gas (GHG) Protocol. The GHG Protocol established a framework for setting boundaries, distinguishing between temporal, organizational, and operational scopes. Temporal boundaries defined a baseline year for measuring progress, while organizational boundaries specified which units would be included, such as based on ownership or control. Operational boundaries defined the sources of emissions to be included, with the concept of "scopes" helping users differentiate between various types of emissions sources. This 3-Scope framework has facilitated clarity in GHG tracking. The GHG Protocol also provided guidance on what constituted a "complete" GHG inventory, requiring mandatory reporting of Scope 1 and 2 emissions and making Scope 3 emissions optional. The Carbon Commitment similarly required reporting of Scope 1 and 2 emissions, as well as a subset of Scope 3 emissions, such as commuting, study abroad, and business travel. ## The Power of Aggregated, Standardized Data Since 2007, over 600 campuses that signed the Carbon Commitment have been reporting their Scope 1, 2, and selected Scope 3 emissions every other year. In addition, other campuses have opted to set their own emissions reduction goals. The aggregation and standardization of this data have enabled a more comprehensive understanding of emissions trends and have facilitated the development of effective strategies for reducing energy consumption and greenhouse gas emissions. ## See Also - [[Greenhouse Gas Protocol]]
