# Transition to a Sustainable Circular Society: More than Just Resource Efficiency

The concept of a circular economy (CE) has emerged as a central theme in contemporary sustainability discourse, offering a framework for rethinking economic systems to align with ecological and social well-being. While CE is often associated with resource efficiency, this opinion paper argues that a true transition to a sustainable circular society requires a broader understanding of socio-ecological cycles and their interconnections. By examining seven key cycles—biogeochemical, ecosystem, resource, power, wealth, knowledge, and care—this paper proposes a more holistic approach to circularity that addresses ecological, social, and political dimensions of sustainability.

## Background

The idea of a circular economy, though rooted in ecological principles from the 1970s, has gained significant traction in recent decades as a response to the socio-ecological crises of the twenty-first century. Academics, public institutions, and private organizations have all contributed to the development and promotion of CE as a solution to issues such as resource scarcity, environmental pollution, and economic revitalization. However, the concept remains contested, with diverse interpretations and competing visions that often prioritize technocratic solutions over broader social and ecological implications. This paper seeks to address these gaps by exploring the metaphor of circularity and its relevance to both economic and societal systems.

## Seven Socio-Ecological Cycles to Understand Circularity

Human societies and the planet operate through a complex interplay of cycles and flows. While there are countless socio-ecological cycles, this paper identifies seven that are particularly relevant to understanding circularity and its implications for sustainability:

1. **Biogeochemical cycles of the Earth**  
These cycles, such as the water, carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus cycles, are fundamental to the circulation and transformation of matter and energy on Earth. They ensure the availability of resources for ecosystems and organisms, and their disruption has significant implications for climate change and food security [^16].

2. **Ecosystem cycles**  
Natural ecosystems function through cycles that enable the continuous reproduction and regeneration of life. Energy flows through ecosystems, starting with sunlight captured by photosynthetic organisms and moving through various trophic levels. Nutrients are continuously cycled in a regenerative manner, with the waste of one species serving as food for another [^21].

3. **Resource cycles of materials and energy**  
These cycles involve the extraction, use, and regeneration of materials and energy. A circular economy seeks to close these loops to minimize waste and maximize resource efficiency.

4. **Political cycles of power**  
Power dynamics shape the distribution of resources and the implementation of policies. Understanding these cycles is essential for addressing issues of equity, control, and decision-making in a circular transition.

5. **Economic cycles of wealth, capital, and money**  
The flow of wealth and capital is central to economic systems. A circular economy must consider how these flows can be restructured to promote sustainability and social equity.

6. **Knowledge cycle of technology, information, and education**  
The production, dissemination, and application of knowledge are critical for driving innovation and informing sustainable practices. This cycle influences how societies approach technological development and education.

7. **Social cycles of care**  
Care is a fundamental aspect of human relationships and societal well-being. The care cycle encompasses the provision of care, its distribution, and its impact on social cohesion and resilience.

These seven cycles are deeply interrelated and should be understood as a holistic framework rather than isolated elements. Their interconnections reflect the complex interactions between ecological, social, and economic systems.
