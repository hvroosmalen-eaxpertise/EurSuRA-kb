# Collaborative Networks in Industry 4.0 and Industry 5.0

Collaborative networks (CNs) are evolving as key enablers of industrial transformation, particularly in the context of Industry 4.0 and the emerging paradigm of Industry 5.0. These networks, composed of autonomous entities such as organizations, people, and smart machines, are central to achieving goals such as sustainability, resilience, and human-centricity. This article provides an overview of the evolution of CNs, their role in digital transformation, and their potential contributions to the transition toward Industry 5.0.

The industrial landscape is undergoing a profound transformation, evolving from Industry 4.0 to Industry 5.0. This process was initially driven by the convergence of multiple technologies, particularly cyber-physical systems (CPS) and the Internet of Things (IoT), which reached a point of maturity. These developments have since been augmented by various so-called \"exponential technologies.\" Political and economic strategies have increasingly emphasized re-industrialization and the revitalization of key sectors through digital transformation. While Industry 4.0 was primarily technology-centric, Industry 5.0 introduces broader priorities, including human-centricity, sustainability, and resilience. Nonetheless, technology continues to play a central role, with growing emphasis on artificial intelligence (AI), hyper-connectivity among systems, machines, organizations, and people, and the need for robust cyber-security.

This increasing hyperconnectivity reinforces the importance of collaboration among networks of autonomous entities, which is captured by the notion of collaborative network (CN). A CN is typically defined as a structure \"composed of a variety of entities—organizations, people, and even smart machines—which are largely autonomous, geographically distributed, and heterogeneous in terms of their operating environment, culture, social capital, and goals ... that collaborate to (better) achieve common or compatible goals\". Originally focused on supply chains, extended enterprises, and virtual enterprises, the CN concept has progressively expanded, now encompassing diverse forms and impacting all dimensions of industrial systems. This evolution is also reflected in various roadmaps and strategic research agendas for advanced manufacturing, which increasingly acknowledge the multifaceted role of CNs.

## Evolution of Collaborative Networks

As technology advances and systems become more integrated and interconnected, diverse models of CNs have emerged. These models represent a variety of organizational forms, support multiple business models, and are facilitated by evolving infrastructures and collaboration platforms. Similar to the classification of industrial revolutions, the development of CNs can be grouped into distinct generations, as initially proposed in Ref. [1].

- **CN 1.0**—the initial phase of CNs, primarily centered on goal-oriented networks. This stage saw the emergence of dynamic supply chains, extended enterprises, virtual enterprises, and virtual organizations.
- **CN 2.0**—marked by the conceptualization of the virtual organization breeding environment as a strategic long-term alliance to facilitate the preparedness of its members to engage in dynamic collaborative consortia. It encompasses business ecosystems, industry clusters, industrial districts, and professional virtual communities, and marks the formal introduction of the term \"CNs.\"
- **CN 3.0**—emphasizing interaction among multiple CNs. It includes hybrid value system networks, co-creation, and open innovation ecosystems (e.g., digital innovation hubs), and explores complex issues such as multi-level membership, transitions and inheritance across networks, and the rise of multi-supplier service-based models.
- **CN 4.0**—extending the concept of collaboration to encompass networks of (smart) CPS, including interactions among digital twins representing physical entities. This stage also introduces the perspective of collaborative networks as systems of distributed cognition and highlights their role in support of resilience.
- **CN 5.0**—reflecting developments along two main directions: ① the emergence of hybrid human–AI CNs, and ② the ability to operate effectively in volatility, uncertainty, complexity, and ambiguity (VUCA) environments. This phase emphasizes advancements in value creation, as well as enhancing resilience and fostering antifragility within collaborative systems.

As this evolution continues, new forms of CNs will naturally emerge, shaped by evolving needs and contextual demands.

## Collaborative Networks in Industry 4.0 and Digital Transformation

As highlighted in previous works (e.g., Refs. [1, 6, 9, 10]), CNs serve as a key enabler of Industry 4.0 and broader digital transformation initiatives. Their role spans all dimensions of Industry 4.0, encompassing both the manufacturing/production systems and product/service perspectives (Fig. 2).

### Manufacturing System Dimensions

At the manufacturing/production systems level, we can consider three sub-dimensions:

1. **Vertical integration**—emphasizing the convergence of physical and digital worlds through CPS, IoT, and the concepts of agentification and digital twins, enabling dynamic, responsive manufacturing ecosystems. At higher organizational levels, the shift is away from large, monolithic systems (e.g., traditional manufacturing execution systems (MES) and enterprise resource planning (ERP)) toward federated, collaborative services. This shift supports a redefined view of the enterprise as a multi-layered CN ecosystem, composed of interconnected smart components and human actors.
2. **Horizontal integration**—focusing collaboration across the value chain—including suppliers, manufacturers, customers, and recyclers. As manufacturing moves from large, centralized facilities to smaller, distributed production units, networking and collaboration among decentralized entities become crucial. Emerging paradigms such as distributed and social manufacturing highlight the importance of inter-organizational collaboration.
3. **Acceleration of manufacturing**—triggered by the rise of \"exponential technologies\" (e.g., robotics, drones, AI, additive manufacturing, sensing, nanotechnologies, neuro-technologies, mobile computing, and big data analytics), which introduce new actors, work practices, and forms of human–machine interaction (e.g., augmented reality (AR), collaborative robotics, and remote interaction), giving rise to novel collaborative modalities.

### Product and Service Dimensions

1. **End-to-end engineering**—focused on integrating all product lifecycle stages, from design to disposal—which requires both internal and external collaboration. Internally, this entails collaboration across departments such as market research, design, and production. Externally, it involves engaging suppliers and customers in co-design and co-innovation, as well as leveraging social networks to \"sense\" market trends. Servicing during use and recycling at end-of-life further extend the scope of collaboration beyond the firm.
2. **Smart products**—encompassing digital modeling and embedding intelligence into products. For instance, features like memory tags enable traceability and support \"stigmergic collaboration\" among stakeholders. Furthermore, the fusion of products and services, as in product-service systems, demands multi-enterprise collaboration.
3. **New business models**—connectivity and digitalization catalyze new business models such as customer-intimate strategies, extended servitization, and service-dominant logic, \"glocal\" operations, hybrid value chains combining social and economic goals, and data-driven services. All these models rely on strong multi-stakeholder collaboration.

In addition, a range of transversal challenges cuts across all these dimensions. One notable example is cyber-security in CPS. The increasing availability of data, fueled by widespread sensor deployment and systems interconnectivity, leads to broad adoption of big data analytics and machine learning.

## Collaborative Networks in Industry 5.0

Industry 5.0 emphasizes new goals towards achieving industrial systems that are (more) sustainable, resilient, and antifragile, intelligent, and human-centered (Fig. 3).

The pillar of Sustainability in Fig. 3 can be well supported by concepts and mechanisms introduced in CN 2.0 and CN 3.0. The other two pillars map well with the concerns and developments of CN 4.0 and especially CN 5.0.

### Collaborative Networks and Sustainability

Sustainability is inherently complex, due to its multi-dimensional nature and the challenge of reconciling often conflicting objectives among diverse stakeholders. In manufacturing, this complexity is reflected in concepts like sustainable manufacturing, industrial symbiosis, and the circular economy.

Effectively implementing these concepts relies on collaboration among involved actors, which underscores the critical role of CNs in enabling sustainable production. For instance, the business ecosystem concept, as in virtual organization breeding environment (VBEs), illustrates shared sustainability responsibility, while circular ecosystems emphasize closed-loop value creation and resource efficiency.

Indeed, across Industry 4.0 dimensions, networks of organizations, individuals, smart machines, and intelligent systems can collaborate toward global efficiency and sustainability. These heterogeneous, autonomous entities pose integration challenges beyond interoperability and control, necessitating environments based on negotiation, contracting, resource sharing, and value co-creation.

Viewing manufacturing systems as networks of (semi-)autonomous components implies distributed responsibility. Consequently, sustainability must be addressed through a CN lens, emphasizing shared accountability and coordinated efforts among all involved participants.

Previous research has largely focused on CNs' contributions to sustainability via the horizontal integration dimension, particularly in the circular economy, industrial symbiosis, and, to some extent, new business models such as hybrid value chains. Other areas remain underexplored, signaling the need for further analysis of trends and examples to identify research gaps.

While Industry 4.0 initially emphasized technological advancement, recent developments increasingly target sustainable manufacturing practices—addressing not only economic factors like cost and profit but also social and environmental aspects. However, the literature offers limited insights into how collaboration directly or indirectly enhances sustainability within industrial ecosystems.

Achieving sustainability requires measurable progress, supported by performance indicators tailored to evaluate collaboration's impact on resilience and sustainability. Though initial efforts have been made, further research is needed to refine these indicators and assess their effectiveness.
