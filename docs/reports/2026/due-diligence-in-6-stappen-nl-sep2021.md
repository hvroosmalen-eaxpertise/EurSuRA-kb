# Due Diligence under the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises

Due diligence, also referred to as [[gepaste zorgvuldigheid]] (appropriate care) or [[ketenverantwoordelijkheid]] (supply chain responsibility), is a continuous process through which enterprises identify, cease, prevent, and mitigate adverse impacts on human rights, labour rights, and the environment.[^1] The process is a central component of [[international corporate social responsibility]] (ICSR) and is embedded in the internationally recognised Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises (2011) and the United Nations Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (UNGPs).[^1] The Dutch National Contact Point (NCP) for the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises has published a six-step framework that operationalises the due diligence process for enterprises.[^1]

---

## Background

The OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises, last revised in 2011, constitute a set of government-backed recommendations addressing responsible business conduct in areas including human rights, employment, the environment, and anti-corruption.[^1] The UNGPs, endorsed by the United Nations Human Rights Council in 2011, establish a complementary framework articulating the respective duties of states and responsibilities of businesses with regard to human rights.[^1] Both instruments place due diligence at the core of responsible enterprise behaviour, requiring companies to identify and address actual and potential adverse impacts arising from their own operations, products, services, and business relationships throughout their value chains.[^1]

The Dutch NCP, operating under the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, serves as the national implementation body for the OECD Guidelines in the Netherlands and provides guidance, mediation, and dispute resolution services to enterprises and affected parties.[^1]

---

## The Six-Step Due Diligence Framework

The NCP has outlined six sequential and iterative steps that constitute the due diligence process.[^1] These steps are described below.

### Step 1: Integrate Corporate Social Responsibility into Policy and Management Systems

Enterprises are expected to develop, explicitly endorse, and publish a corporate social responsibility (CSR) policy based on the OECD Guidelines.[^1] Where a CSR policy already exists, it should be reviewed and updated periodically.[^1] The policy must be integrated into the enterprise's management structure and oversight bodies so that it becomes embedded in regular business processes; employee training is considered an integral part of this integration.[^1] CSR expectations should additionally be communicated to suppliers and other business relations.[^1]

### Step 2: Identify and Assess Actual and Potential Adverse Impacts

Enterprises are required to conduct a CSR risk analysis mapping business activities and relationships with respect to actual or potential adverse impacts.[^1] The analysis is distinguished from conventional enterprise risk assessments by its focus on risks to affected stakeholders—such as workers or local communities—rather than risks to the enterprise itself.[^1] On the basis of the risk analysis, the most serious adverse impacts are to be identified and prioritised in order of significance; smaller enterprises with less complex value chains may, in some cases, be able to identify the principal adverse impacts directly.[^1] The analysis further requires determining the nature of the enterprise's involvement in identified impacts—whether the enterprise causes, contributes to, or is directly linked to an adverse impact through a business relationship.[^1] Risk prioritisation informs the sequence in which adverse impacts are to be addressed.[^1]

### Step 3: Cease, Prevent, and Mitigate Adverse Impacts

Enterprises are expected to cease business activities that cause or contribute to adverse impacts.[^1] Plans should be developed to prevent and reduce potential future adverse impacts, based on the previously established risk prioritisation.[^1] Where an enterprise is directly linked to an adverse impact through a business relationship, several responses are available: the relationship may be continued, temporarily suspended, or terminated following unsuccessful attempts at mitigation.[^1] Termination is considered a last resort; the enterprise is expected to make maximum efforts towards improvement before discontinuing a relationship, and the potential negative social and economic consequences of termination are to be weighed in such decisions.[^1] ICSR expectations for suppliers, customers, and other business relations should be clearly formalised.[^1]

### Step 4: Monitor the Practical Application and Results of Measures

Enterprises are expected to monitor periodically the implementation and effectiveness of due diligence measures taken.[^1] Such measures encompass the identification, prevention, and mitigation of adverse impacts as well as the provision of access to remedy.[^1] Business relations are also to be assessed to verify that measures reducing adverse risks are being implemented.[^1] The conclusions drawn from monitoring activities should be used to improve the overall due diligence process.[^1]

### Step 5: Communicate How Adverse Impacts Are Being Addressed

Enterprises are expected to make their CSR policy publicly available and to be transparent about the measures taken to address adverse impacts, including an account of their effectiveness.[^1] Appropriate communication channels include an annual report or other public disclosures on sustainability or responsible entrepreneurship.[^1] Information is to be published in an accessible manner.[^1]

### Step 6: Provide for or Cooperate in Remediation

Where an enterprise has caused or contributed to adverse impacts, it is expected to provide access to remedy or to cooperate in remediation.[^1] Affected parties should be able to submit complaints through a grievance mechanism established by the enterprise.[^1] Disputes regarding the application of the OECD Guidelines—including disagreements over whether an adverse impact has occurred or over the appropriate remedy—may be submitted to an official remediation mechanism such as the NCP, which offers mediation services for this purpose.[^1]
