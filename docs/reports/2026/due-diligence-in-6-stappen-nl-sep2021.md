# Due Diligence in Corporate Sustainability: A Six-Step Framework

Due diligence, in the context of international corporate responsibility, is a continuous process through which companies identify, prevent, and mitigate actual and potential negative impacts on human rights, labour rights, and the environment. [^1] The framework is recognised as a core element of internationally responsible business conduct (Dutch: *internationaal maatschappelijk verantwoord ondernemen*, IMVO) and occupies a central place in the [[OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises]] (2011) and the [[United Nations Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights]] (UNGPs). [^1] The process is structured around six sequential, iterative steps designed to embed responsible conduct into corporate management systems and supply chain relationships.

---

## Background

The concept of due diligence as applied to sustainability and human rights originates from two internationally recognised instruments: the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises, updated in 2011, and the United Nations Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (UNGPs). [^1] Both frameworks establish expectations for companies to identify and address adverse impacts associated with their operations, products, and services, including those arising through business relationships in the value chain. [^1]

In the Netherlands, the National Contact Point (NCP) for the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises, administered by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, promotes application of the framework among Dutch companies and serves as an official mediation mechanism for disputes concerning the Guidelines. [^1] The due diligence concept is also referred to in Dutch-language contexts as *gepaste zorgvuldigheid* (appropriate care) or *ketenverantwoordelijkheid* (chain responsibility). [^1]

A comprehensive description of the process is contained in the OECD Due Diligence Guidance for Responsible Business Conduct. [^1]

## The Six-Step Due Diligence Process

### Step 1: Integrate Corporate Social Responsibility into Policy and Management Systems

The first step requires companies to develop, explicitly endorse, and publish a corporate social responsibility (CSR) policy grounded in the OECD Guidelines. [^1] Existing CSR policies should be reviewed and updated periodically. [^1] The policy must be integrated into the company's management structure and governing bodies so that it becomes part of regular business processes, and employee training should be provided to support implementation. [^1] CSR expectations are also to be incorporated into engagements with suppliers and other business relationships. [^1]

### Step 2: Identify and Assess Actual and Potential Adverse Impacts

Companies are expected to conduct a CSR risk analysis that maps business activities and business relationships with respect to actual or potential adverse impacts. [^1] Crucially, the focus of this analysis is on risks to affected stakeholders — such as workers or local communities — rather than risks to the company itself. [^1]

On the basis of the risk analysis, companies should determine the most serious adverse impacts and prioritise them in order of severity. [^1] Smaller enterprises operating less complex value chains may be able to identify the most significant impacts more directly. [^1] The analysis also requires companies to determine the nature of their involvement: a company may cause an adverse impact directly, contribute to one, or be directly linked to an impact through a business relationship. [^1] Prioritisation of involvement type then determines which impacts are to be addressed first. [^1]

### Step 3: Stop, Prevent, and Mitigate Adverse Impacts

Where business activities cause or contribute to adverse impacts, those activities should cease. [^1] Companies are additionally required to develop plans to prevent and reduce potential future adverse impacts, in line with established risk prioritisation. [^1]

Where a company is directly linked to an adverse impact through a business relationship, several responses are available: the relationship may be continued, temporarily suspended, or terminated following unsuccessful attempts to reduce the impact. [^1] It is considered essential, however, that a company make maximum efforts to improve the situation before choosing to terminate a relationship, and that the potential adverse social and economic consequences of termination are taken into account in that decision. [^1] CSR expectations for suppliers, customers, and other business relationships should be clearly defined and documented. [^1]

### Step 4: Monitor Implementation and Results

Companies are required to monitor periodically the implementation and effectiveness of their due diligence measures. [^1] Such measures encompass the identification, prevention, and mitigation of adverse impacts as well as the provision of access to remedy. [^1] Business relationships should also be assessed to verify that impact-mitigation measures are being carried out. [^1] The findings of monitoring exercises are to be used to improve the overall due diligence process. [^1]

### Step 5: Communicate How Adverse Impacts Are Being Addressed

Companies are expected to make their CSR policy public and to be transparent about the measures taken to address adverse impacts, including an account of their effectiveness. [^1] Appropriate channels include annual reports or other publicly available sustainability or responsible business reporting. [^1] This information should be published in an accessible manner. [^1]

### Step 6: Provide for or Cooperate in Remediation

Where a company has caused or contributed to adverse impacts, it is required to provide access to remedy or to cooperate in remediation efforts. [^1] Companies should ensure that affected parties can submit complaints through a grievance mechanism. [^1]

Where disagreement exists regarding the application of the OECD Guidelines — for example, over whether a company caused an adverse impact or over the appropriate remedy — the matter may be submitted to an official remedy mechanism such as the NCP. [^1] The NCP offers mediation services and is particularly relevant when the parties involved cannot reach agreement independently. [^1]
