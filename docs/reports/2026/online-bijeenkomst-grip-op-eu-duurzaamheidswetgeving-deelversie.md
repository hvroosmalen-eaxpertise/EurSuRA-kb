# EU Sustainability Legislation: An Integrated Compliance Framework

EU sustainability legislation encompasses a growing body of European Union directives and regulations that impose due diligence, reporting, and product-specific obligations on companies operating within or supplying to the European market. An integrated approach to this legislation—drawing on the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) six-step due diligence framework as a common foundation—enables companies to reduce duplicated effort, manage supply chain risks, and fulfil multiple regulatory obligations through a single coherent process.[^1]

---

## Background

From approximately 2021 onward, the European Union has progressively enacted a suite of sustainability-related laws covering corporate reporting, human rights, environmental protection, forced labour, deforestation, battery supply chains, and conflict minerals.[^1] Each instrument carries its own objectives, supervisory authorities, and implementation timelines. According to RBConnect and Future Up, companies have historically approached these laws as separate compliance trajectories, thereby duplicating effort and generating unnecessary regulatory burden.[^1] A pilot project commissioned under the Dutch Ministry of Economic Affairs (Economische Zaken, EZ) examined how companies could fulfil their obligations under multiple laws simultaneously through an integrated implementation methodology.[^1]

---

## Regulatory Landscape

### Due Diligence Legislation

**Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (CSDDD)**
The CSDDD requires companies to implement due diligence across their value chains as an integral element of business operations, covering all human rights—including the five fundamental principles and rights at work—as well as negative environmental impacts and impacts related to human well-being. The directive applies from July 2029.[^1]

**Forced Labour Regulation (FLR)**
The FLR prohibits the import, sale, and export of products made with forced or compulsory labour. Its scope encompasses upstream supply chain relationships. Implementation is anticipated from December 2027.[^1]

### Risk- and Product-Specific Legislation

**EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR)**
The EUDR prohibits the import of products and their derivatives that have been produced on deforested or forest-degraded land. Covered commodities include beef, cocoa, coffee, palm oil, rubber, soya, and wood. The regulation entered into force in December 2026.[^1]

**EU Battery Regulation (EUBR)**
The EUBR establishes due diligence requirements for nickel, cobalt, graphite, lithium, and their related chemical compounds. It addresses risks in the areas of environment, climate, and human health; human rights; labour rights and labour relations; and community life, including indigenous peoples. The regulation applies from August 2027.[^1]

**Conflict Minerals Regulation (CMR)**
The CMR obliges importers to conduct and report due diligence on 3TG minerals (tin, tantalum, tungsten, and gold). It has been in force since 2021.[^1]

### Transparency and Reporting Legislation

**Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD)**
The CSRD requires companies to publish annual sustainability information in accordance with the European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS). It covers environmental, social (including human rights), and governance (ESG) matters across the full value chain, both upstream and downstream. The directive applies from 2025.[^1]

**EU Taxonomy Regulation**
The EU Taxonomy Regulation provides a classification system for sustainable economic activities and is linked to corporate reporting obligations. It has applied since 2025.[^1]

## Common Structure of EU Sustainability Obligations

Despite their differing subject matter, the legislative instruments above share a common structural logic. According to RBConnect, the fundamental questions posed by most EU sustainability laws are:[^1]

1. Gaining insight into supply chains and associated risks;
2. Taking measures to prevent, limit, or remedy identified risks and impacts;
3. Collecting and, where required, reporting relevant information; and
4. Being able to substantiate the choices made.

The shared conceptual foundation is **due diligence**—defined as a continuous process through which companies identify, prevent, limit, stop, and remediate risks to human rights and the environment and climate in their value chains, and account for the actions taken.[^1]

### Key Characteristics of Due Diligence

The OECD Due Diligence Guidance for Responsible Business Conduct, which underpins several of the directives listed above, identifies the following characteristics of due diligence:[^1]

- Grounded in internationally recognised corporate social responsibility (CSR) norms;
- Dynamic in nature;
- Proportionate to the scale and context of a company's operations;
- Risk-oriented;
- Preventive in focus;
- Attentive to gender and vulnerable groups;
- Requires stakeholder engagement; and
- Based on shared responsibility across the value chain.

### The OECD Six-Step Framework

The OECD Due Diligence Guidance structures due diligence as a six-step process. According to the pilot project analysis, this framework serves as the shared compliance basis across the CSDDD, CSRD, EUDR, FLR, EUBR, and CMR:[^1]

| Step | Description |
|------|-------------|
| 1 | Embed due diligence into company policy and management systems |
| 2 | Identify and assess adverse impacts in operations and the value chain |
| 3 | Cease, prevent, or mitigate adverse impacts |
| 4 | Track implementation and results |
| 5 | Communicate how impacts are addressed |
| 6 | Provide for or cooperate in remediation where appropriate |

Collaboration with supply chain partners, sector initiatives, and stakeholders is identified as integral to all six steps.[^1]

## Integrated Implementation Approach

### Rationale

The pilot project identified eight principal benefits of an integrated, rather than law-by-law, approach to compliance:[^1]

1. Reduction of duplicated work across departments;
2. Lower operational and information technology costs;
3. Decisions grounded in supply chain insight;
4. Improved supply chain and risk management;
5. Greater long-term regulatory resilience;
6. Measurable impact in supply chains;
7. Alignment with the company's own sustainability ambitions; and
8. Consistent external communication and reporting.

### Phase 1: Integrated Prioritisation

The first phase of the integrated roadmap involves identifying and prioritising supply chains for further investigation. Starting from a baseline understanding of a company's products, raw materials, and packaging, the process applies the following sequential assessments:[^1]

1. **Regulatory scope:** Do the products or materials fall under market-access legislation (EUDR, FLR), product- or risk-oriented legislation (EUBR, CMR), or other sustainability legislation (CSRD, CSDDD, Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR), Ecodesign Regulation)?
2. **Customer requirements:** Are products, materials, or risks subject to contractual customer demands?
3. **Dependency and materiality:** Is the product or material essential to the business, or significant by volume or value?
4. **Known high risks:** Are there established indicators of elevated human rights or environmental risk?
5. **Existing risk management:** Are there strong signals that risks are already being managed effectively?

Supply chains that pass through all five assessments without being selected for further investigation may be provisionally set aside for a later due diligence cycle.[^1]

### Phase 2: Integrated Governance and Implementation

The second phase establishes the organisational infrastructure for ongoing due diligence. Key steps include:[^1]

- Establishing light-touch governance, including a designated lead and small core team;
- Conducting an internal awareness session to create organisational buy-in;
- Completing a gap analysis to assess the extent to which the six OECD steps are already being applied and to identify which regulatory obligations apply to which priority supply chains;
- Holding a vision and mission workshop to align sustainability ambitions with strategic choices;
- Formalising governance structures, including roles per department, consultation structures, and escalation lines;
- Establishing a policy framework, including a CSR policy and a two-way code of conduct; and
- Integrating due diligence into existing systems and developing new procedures where required.[^1]

### Collaborative Due Diligence

Collaboration is identified as a fundamental element of both due diligence and the underlying legislation. Because companies are dependent on others in their supply chains, individual action is considered insufficient; sector-level and multi-stakeholder collaboration is presented as necessary for achieving systemic change.[^1]

## Value Chain Scope Across Instruments

Each regulatory instrument defines a specific value chain scope. The following summarises the upstream and downstream reach of the primary instruments:[^1]

| Instrument | Upstream scope | Downstream scope |
|------------|---------------|-----------------|
| CSRD | Yes | Yes |
| CSDDD | Yes | Yes |
| EUBR | Yes | Yes |
| EUDR | Yes | Limited |
| FLR | Yes | Yes |
| OECD Guidelines | Yes | Yes |

## Beyond Compliance: Strategic Opportunities

The pilot project and associated sessions identify six categories of strategic opportunity arising from compliance with EU sustainability legislation, beyond the avoidance of regulatory penalties:[^1]

1. **Market access:** Sustainability and supply chain transparency are increasingly prerequisites for public procurement and business-to-business contracting.
2. **Commercial opportunity:** Supply chain insight may reveal possibilities to improve or innovate product and service offerings.
3. **Financing conditions:** Demonstrated risk management and supply chain insight improve the prospects of obtaining financing and favourable terms.
4. **Strategic decision-making:** Enhanced data supports better-informed investment, procurement, and long-term planning decisions.
5. **Risk management and business continuity:** Companies become less vulnerable to supply chain disruptions and market shifts.
6. **Targeted impact:** Companies can direct sustainability efforts toward the issues where they can create the most meaningful change.
