# An Architecture for Sustainable Value Transition within Social and Planetary Boundaries

## Lead

The European Financial Reporting Advisory Group ([[EFRAG (European Financial Reporting Advisory Group)]]) published *An Architecture for Sustainable Value Transition within Social and Planetary Boundaries*, proposing a systemic framework for realigning corporate and economic activity with ecological and social limits.[^1] The paper introduces two central concepts: a **Sustainable Value Transition** (SVT), defined as a fundamental reorientation of the way value is defined, planned, and rewarded; and a **Sustainable Value Architecture** (SVA), the set of institutional structures and mechanisms needed to support that transition.[^1] The authors argue that prevailing forms of capitalism place accelerating pressure on ecological and social systems, and that conventional finance and economic theory contain simplifications that prevent the necessary alignment between financial and wider forms of value.[^1] Nine interdependent components of the SVA are proposed, spanning corporate governance, law, reporting, product information, supply-chain transparency, macroeconomic accounts, fiscal policy, and education.[^1]

---

## Background

### Context and motivation

Current social and environmental trends—including accelerating social inequality and environmental degradation—are characterised in the paper as evidence that the prevailing mode of capitalism is straining an increasingly failing system.[^1] Traditional finance and economic theory are identified as suffering from simplifications that do not adequately reflect the complexity of the challenges faced by corporations, investors, and regulators.[^1]

The paper argues that addressing and reversing these trends requires an evolution of system function and purpose: a move beyond exclusive shareholder-value creation towards a new form of sustainable stakeholder value that more effectively represents, balances, and aligns currently priced, underpriced, and unpriced forms of capital.[^1] The term **multiple capitals** refers to the range of asset types—financial, manufactured, natural, human, social, and intellectual—whose combined state determines long-run societal welfare.[^1]

### Intended audience

The paper is directed at regulators, systems architects, academics, sustainability practitioners, and activists. It is intended to contribute analysis and to support discussion about aligning economic and corporate intent, activity, and impact with the limitations of a finite planet and the requirements of society.[^1]

### Relationship to the climate response

The paper situates SVT within the broader context of the emerging global response to the climate crisis, observing that global challenges require coordinated responses combining bottom-up approaches (such as individual organisations setting net-zero carbon goals and trajectories) with top-down approaches (such as international conventions, agreed data sets, and shared typologies).[^1]

## Academic Foundations

Section 2 of the paper, supplemented by an appendix, summarises the academic literature underpinning SVT. The paper identifies crucial simplifications in mainstream financial and economic theory and draws on scholarly work concerning [[planetary boundaries]], stakeholder theory, ecological economics, and transition management to articulate why structural change is required.[^1] Readers seeking greater depth are directed to the paper's appendix, which provides an extended treatment of these academic foundations.[^1]

## Four Strategic Goals for a Sustainable Value Transition

The paper proposes four additive strategic goals that together constitute the success criteria for an SVT, contrasting current practice with ideal practice in each dimension.[^1]

| Strategic Goal | Current Practice | Ideal Practice |
|---|---|---|
| **Managing for the long term** | Short-term focus visible in incentives, actions, and communications, often at the cost of long-term outcomes | Clear view of long-term goals; short-term actions verifiably aligned with long-term goals |
| **Societal and shareholder value creation** | Predominant shareholder-value creation focus at the expense of social and ecological value; emergent and marginally adopted systems for sustainable value management | Societal value creation measured, managed, and accounted for as rigorously as financial value |
| **Within social and planetary boundaries** | Companies do not know the extent to which they operate inside or outside social and planetary boundaries | Companies operate within social and planetary boundaries, for example with a clear and declining carbon budget |
| **Along transition pathways** | Transition thinking largely absent from strategic discussions; lack of phase-out strategies | Transition thinking integral to both strategic discussions and operational decisions; all system participants share an understanding of limits and impacts |

[^1]

## Nine Components of the Sustainable Value Architecture

The SVA is described as a set of conditions, mechanisms, and institutions that work in combination to enable and utilise the flow of essential sustainability data. The stated purposes are to: place companies and investors in a shared information context; support decision-making for sustainable outcomes; and align incentives and rewards for sustainable behaviour.[^1]

From a corporate management perspective, components 1, 4, 5, and 6 are considered substantially within an organisation's sphere of control and are identified as forming the basis of a management dashboard that would make corporate performance in environmental and social dimensions visible, measurable, and manageable.[^1]

### 1. Corporate Objectives, Policies, and Systems

This component calls for the development of corporate purpose with an explicit focus on the creation of balanced, long-term, sustainable value, fully integrated and reinforced through embedding in corporate systems.[^1]

### 2. Ownership and Control

Companies are encouraged to adopt a more strategic approach to their shareholder base, seeking alignment between investors with a long-term focus on the corporate mission and a free float of non-committed shareholders.[^1]

### 3. Corporate Law

The paper advocates for corporate law and [[fiduciary duty|fiduciary duties]] that explicitly align with the delivery of long-term sustainable value creation, rather than being interpreted narrowly as obligations to maximise short-term financial returns.[^1]

### 4. Corporate Reporting

This component proposes balanced sustainability disclosure built around standardised core content, enabling consistent reporting of organisational [[externalities]] and signalling value creation or destruction across multiple capitals.[^1]

### 5. Product Information

Transparent, comparable, and verifiable product-level social and environmental impact information is identified as a necessary condition for informed consumption and investment decisions.[^1]

### 6. Supply Chain Reporting

Interoperability of product-level information is described as enabling different parties within supply chains, as well as third parties, to collate and assemble complete pictures of sustainable impact and value.[^1]

### 7. Global and National Accounts and Statistics

Global and national accounts are characterised as the "connective tissue" between true prices, product information, supply-chain reporting, and the taxation and pricing of externalities, allowing a global picture to be assembled of state and trend information relative to planetary boundaries and social conditions.[^1]

### 8. Taxation and the Pricing of Externalities

The paper proposes a shift in the focus and purpose of taxation: away from labour and towards materials; taxing negative externalities at source; and removing or reducing incentives for environmentally and socially harmful activity.[^1]

### 9. Business School and University Education

Reform of business school and university curricula is identified as essential to embedding the values, knowledge, and analytical tools required for managing within social and planetary boundaries in the next generation of decision-makers.[^1]

## Obstacles to Managing for Sustainable Value Transition

Section 4 of the paper identifies obstacles that may impede the development of the SVA and explores potential solutions from the perspective of different actor categories—including corporations, investors, regulators, and governments.[^1] The paper does not present these obstacles as insurmountable but argues that their resolution requires coordinated action across the nine architectural components.[^1]

---

## Information Flows

Section 5 addresses the information flows required to enable a sustainable transition. The paper argues that for the various actors constituting the value chain to act in concert, architecture must outline mechanisms for processing global information on environmental and social conditions, performance in terms of impact and usage, and the nature and dynamics of relationships between these conditions and corporate activity.[^1] Such information flows would facilitate the assessment and pricing of actors' and products' alignment with social and planetary boundaries.[^1]

---

## Resilience

Section 6 focuses briefly on resilience, understood in the paper as the capacity of social and ecological systems—and the corporations embedded within them—to absorb disturbance and reorganise while undergoing change so as to retain essentially the same function, structure, and identity.[^1] Resilience is presented as both an objective of SVT and a property that the SVA is designed to strengthen.[^1]

---

## Transitions Perspective

Section 7 examines SVT through a transitions-management lens, arguing that transition thinking is an integral but currently largely absent element of both strategic and operational decision-making in most organisations.[^1] The paper calls for explicit phase-out strategies for activities incompatible with social and planetary boundaries, and for the incorporation of transition pathways into corporate governance and reporting frameworks.[^1]

---

## Next Steps

Section 8 outlines potential next steps for establishing the SVA. The paper presents these as requiring action at multiple levels simultaneously—corporate, national, and international—and proposes that the nine architectural components be developed in an integrated manner rather than sequentially.[^1]

---

## Conclusions

The paper concludes that for economic and financial systems to recognise and integrate wider value concepts, significant change is required—not only by and within corporations but also in the institutional context in which they operate.[^1] The authors argue that only when such changes are enacted will the various actors constituting the value chain be able to act in concert to recognise and reward a transition to sustainable value.[^1]

The paper further contends that if new forms of capitalism are to be successful, companies and investors must be able to understand and respond to the needs of stakeholders and a fragile, finite planet, and that a clear conception of sustainable transition, and the architecture which might support it, is essential to achieving this.[^1]
