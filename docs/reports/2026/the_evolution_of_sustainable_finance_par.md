--- # The Evolution of Sustainable Finance Paradigms in Banking: Transitioning from Green Banking to Climate Risk Governance The global banking sector is undergoing a significant transformation, shifting from voluntary environmental commitments to mandatory climate risk governance embedded in prudential supervision. This transition has implications not only for financial stability but also for the resilience of climate-vulnerable communities and micro-enterprises. This article explores the evolution of sustainable finance paradigms in banking, focusing on the shift from green banking to climate risk governance, and its impact on financial inclusion and social equity. ## Background The global banking sector is at an inflection point, moving from a voluntary, operationally framed commitment to environmental stewardship toward a mandatory architecture of climate risk governance embedded in prudential supervision. However, the literature has said little about what this shift means for the smallholders and micro-enterprises who depend on bank credit. ## Theoretical Foundations: Sustainable Banking Typologies and the Triple Bottom Line The theoretical starting point for this paper is Jeucken's (2001) four-stage typology of bank environmental strategy, defensive, preventive, offensive, and sustainable banking, which remains the most widely cited framework for classifying how far a bank has moved from treating environmental regulation as a cost to treating it as a source of competitive advantage. Bouma, Jeucken, and Klinkers (2001) locate the same tension, between defensive compliance and proactive value creation, at the centre of bank environmental strategy more broadly, and Elkington's (1997) Triple Bottom Line, economic, environmental, and social performance judged together, supplies the normative floor beneath all four stages. Applied specifically to banking, Scholtens (2009) makes a distinction this paper leans on throughout: a bank's own operational footprint, its paper use, its energy bills, is almost trivial next to the footprint embedded in its loan portfolio, the sectors and borrowers it chooses to finance. Weber, Scholz, and Michalik (2010) add that folding sustainability criteria into credit risk management reshapes a bank's internal architecture only gradually, through how credit is actually underwritten rather than through what appears in annual reports. Read together, this literature explains why environmental banking policy took as long as it did to reach the loan book: the theoretical vocabulary for talking about portfolio-embedded risk existed by the early 2000s, but the practical machinery for pricing and monitoring it did not. ## International Regulatory Architecture and Institutional Frameworks Institutionally, the scaffolding behind that machinery has been built up in stages since the early 1990s. UNEP-FI, founded in 1992, evolved from a soft-law signatory network into a genuine standard-setter through its Principles for Responsible Banking (UNEP-FI, 2019); its practical contribution to credit analysis, the Environmental and Social Risk Analysis (ESRA) methodology developed through the Ecobanking Project, structures due diligence around project categorisation, detailed assessment against IFC Performance Standards, and ongoing covenant monitoring (Rojas, 2010; IFC, 2012). The Equator Principles, adopted in 2003 and now in their fourth iteration, require signatory banks to categorise project-finance transactions by environmental and social risk before a loan is approved (Equator Principles Association, 2020), and Scholtens and Dam (2007) show that adoption is associated with measurably different lending behaviour rather than symbolic compliance alone. Climate-specific reporting sits on top of this older architecture: the Taskforce on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD, 2017) supplies the four-pillar disclosure structure now mandatory across several major jurisdictions, and the Network for Greening the Financial System (NGFS), founded in 2017 and 144 members strong by 2025, has built the climate scenarios that translate disclosure into actionable risk management practices (NGFS, 2024, 2025). ## Results The shift from green banking to climate risk governance is best read as a qualitative rupture rather than a linear refinement, and Indonesia's regulatory trajectory mirrors that global current; even so, implementation still lags leading international practice, and inclusive outcomes for micro-enterprise borrowers, visible in Bank Rakyat Indonesia's own portfolio, remain a work in progress. ## See Also - [[Green banking]]
