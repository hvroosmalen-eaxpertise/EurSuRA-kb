--- # International Law and Climate-Induced Migration: Protection Without Status Climate-induced migration presents one of the most pressing humanitarian challenges of the twenty-first century, driven by rising sea levels, desertification, extreme weather events, and resource scarcity. These factors have caused millions to abandon their homes, yet international law lacks a coherent framework to protect such populations. The 1951 Refugee Convention, which forms the basis of refugee law, does not contemplate persecution related to climate factors, leaving climate-displaced persons in a legal vacuum. Human rights mechanisms also provide limited protection, failing to address the scale and complexity of climate-induced displacement. The disconnect between legal structures and the realities of climate-induced migration becomes increasingly stark as climate change accelerates, leading to governance failures and threats to human security and international social order. The article by Rafael Leal-Arcas, Faten Alamri, Lobna Alsrraj, Reema Al Sahil, and Sulaiman Almojil examines the capacity of international law to respond to environmentally driven mobility. It explores the interaction between refugee law, international human rights law, disaster governance instruments, and the climate regime. The authors argue that the 1951 Refugee Convention, rooted in a persecution-based paradigm, systematically excludes climate-displaced persons. Human rights-based protections, such as non-refoulement obligations, operate only as ex-post, individualized, and threshold-dependent safeguards. The absence of a defined legal status for climate-displaced persons generates normative indeterminacy, institutional fragmentation, and inconsistent state practice. Drawing on comparative case studies from the Pacific Islands, Bangladesh, and the Sahel, the article demonstrates how these doctrinal limitations translate into protection gaps across both internal and cross-border displacement contexts. This reflects broader tensions between sovereignty and responsibility in international law. The authors reject the viability of comprehensive treaty reform and instead advocate for a doctrinally incremental approach that relies on the reinterpretation of existing norms, the consolidation of complementary protection regimes, and the juridical integration of climate adaptation mechanisms. They conclude that such an approach offers the most legally coherent pathway for extending protection within the current structure of international law. ## Background Climate-induced migration is a phenomenon that arises from environmental changes such as rising sea levels, desertification, extreme weather events, and resource scarcity. These factors have led to the displacement of millions of people, creating significant humanitarian challenges. The term "refugee" is often used interchangeably with "migrant" in everyday language, but they are distinct. A refugee is a legally protected category, while a migrant is broader and non-status-based, largely governed by state discretion. The 1951 Refugee Convention, which forms the basis of refugee law, does not contemplate persecution related to climate factors, leaving climate-displaced persons without legal protection. Human rights mechanisms also provide limited protection, failing to address the scale and complexity of climate-induced displacement. ## Existing Legal Frameworks, Actors, and Finance in Climate Migration The existing legal frameworks, actors, and finance in climate migration are critical to understanding the current state of international law's response to climate-induced migration. International protection frameworks, including the 1951 Refugee Convention, do not provide a coherent structure for protecting climate-displaced persons. Institutional actors, such as the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) and the International Organization for Migration (IOM), play operational and analytical roles in addressing climate-induced migration. However, these institutions face challenges in providing consistent and effective protection due to fragmented mandates and limited resources. Financing instruments and adaptation funds are essential for addressing the needs of climate-displaced persons. These funds aim to support adaptation and mitigation efforts, but their effectiveness is often limited by political and economic constraints. Regional frameworks, such as the Kampala Convention on internal displacement in Africa and the Cartagena Declaration on refugee protection in Latin America, provide patchwork solutions that are not universally applicable. National frameworks, including immigration laws, disaster law, and land regulation, also play a role in addressing climate-induced migration, but they are often inconsistent and lack a unified approach. ## Governance Gaps, Policy Debates, and Saudi Arabiaâ€™s Perspective The governance gaps, policy debates, and Saudi Arabiaâ€™s perspective on climate-induced migration highlight the challenges in addressing this issue within the current legal framework.
