--- # Solar Powered Net Zero Energy Houses for Southern Europe Solar powered net zero energy houses (NZEH) for southern Europe represent a promising approach to achieving energy sustainability in residential buildings. This study investigates the feasibility of integrating solar energy systems into a typical single-family home in a mild southern European climate, with a focus on dynamic thermal simulation and system sizing to meet annual energy demands. The research highlights the potential of solar thermal (ST) and photovoltaic (PV) systems, along with the impact of building design, occupant behavior, and appliance efficiency on energy performance. The findings suggest that solar NZEH systems can be both financially and environmentally viable in this climate zone. ## Background Energy consumption in buildings accounts for approximately 40% of the European Union's final energy use, making building energy efficiency a key policy priority. The concept of a Zero Energy Building (ZEB) has emerged as a response to this challenge, aiming to balance annual energy consumption with on-site renewable energy production. A ZEB can be either grid-connected or off-grid, with the latter being technically and economically challenging due to seasonal mismatches between energy demand and supply. A grid-connected ZEB, referred to as a Net Zero Energy Building (NZEB), allows for energy exchange with the electrical grid, enabling surplus energy to be injected and deficits to be met through grid supply. For residential buildings, this concept is commonly termed a Net Zero Energy Home (NZEH). The development of NZEH builds on the research and experimental validation of Low Energy Homes (LEHs), which began in the 1940s and have gained momentum in recent decades, particularly in the United States and Germany. The German Passivhaus approach, characterized by superinsulation and controlled mechanical ventilation, has been adapted for southern Europe through projects such as Passive-On, which addresses summer overheating. The model house used in this study incorporates moderate insulation, passive and active solar gains, external shading, thermal mass, and natural ventilation, reflecting strategies employed in warmer climates such as the United States and Australia. ## Solar Powered Net Zero Energy Houses The study focuses on the feasibility of solar-powered NZEHs in southern Europe, where high solar irradiation makes solar energy a viable renewable resource. The proposed system combines solar thermal panels with a water tank for thermal inertia and a grid-connected photovoltaic (PV) array. The size of the solar thermal system varies depending on the energy demand, ranging from systems designed to meet only domestic hot water needs to larger combisystems that also provide space heating. Water-based thermal inertia is the most common approach, although alternatives such as seasonal heat storage systems using evacuated tube collectors and soil-based storage have been explored. The integration of photovoltaic systems with thermal energy collection presents additional opportunities for energy efficiency. For instance, a system combining geothermal heat pumps with integrated PV panels can collect both thermal and electrical energy, although it is less efficient than traditional solar thermal systems. The study emphasizes the importance of using currently available technologies, rather than relying on future optimization capabilities, which are discussed in detail by Kolokotsa et al.
