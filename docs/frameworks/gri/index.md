# GRI — Global Reporting Initiative

The Global Reporting Initiative (GRI) is an independent, international organisation that develops the world's most widely used sustainability reporting framework. It was founded in 1997 by the Boston-based Coalition for Environmentally Responsible Economies (Ceres) and the Tellus Institute, in partnership with the United Nations Environment Programme, and is now headquartered in Amsterdam. The GRI Standards offer a modular, free-to-use framework that enables any organisation — business, public institution, or non-profit — to report its most significant impacts on the economy, environment, and people.

GRI reporting is voluntary globally but has become the de-facto baseline: more than three-quarters of the world's largest 250 companies apply the GRI Standards, and many jurisdictions reference GRI in national reporting requirements.

## The 2021 Universal Standards Revision

The GRI Standards were comprehensively restructured in 2021 and apply to reports published for periods beginning on or after 1 January 2023. Three categories of standards work together:

- **Universal Standards** apply to every organisation.
    - **GRI 1: Foundation 2021** — the entry point. Nine reporting principles (accuracy, balance, clarity, comparability, completeness, sustainability context, timeliness, verifiability, and representation of stakeholders) and the requirements for reporting *in accordance with* the GRI Standards.
    - **GRI 2: General Disclosures 2021** — organisation-wide disclosures covering activities and workers, governance, strategy, policies and practices, and stakeholder engagement.
    - **GRI 3: Material Topics 2021** — the process for determining material topics and reporting on how each material topic is managed. Every reporting organisation must publish a list of its material topics and manage disclosures against each.

- **Sector Standards** apply to organisations in specific sectors. The programme is rolling out progressively: GRI 11 (Oil & Gas) in 2021, GRI 12 (Coal) in 2022, GRI 13 (Agriculture, Aquaculture and Fishing) in 2022, GRI 14 (Mining) in 2024, with further sectors in development. Each sector standard lists topics likely to be material for that sector and the disclosures to apply.

- **Topic Standards** cover specific sustainability topics such as GRI 305 Emissions, GRI 403 Occupational Health and Safety, GRI 414 Supplier Social Assessment. Organisations select the Topic Standards relevant to their material topics.

## Impact Materiality — the "Inside-Out" Lens

GRI's materiality concept is **impact materiality**: an organisation reports on the topics that reflect its most significant actual and potential impacts on the economy, environment and people, including impacts on human rights. This is the "inside-out" direction — the organisation's effect on the world. It is distinct from the "outside-in" financial materiality used by the [[International Sustainability Standards Board]] (IFRS S1 / S2) and sits as one half of the [[Double Materiality]] used by the [[European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS)]].

The GRI 3 process is deliberately bottom-up: understand the organisation's context, identify actual and potential impacts across activities and business relationships, assess their significance (severity × likelihood for negative impacts; scale × scope for positive impacts), and prioritise. Stakeholder engagement informs each step without replacing the organisation's own assessment.

## Claims and Levels of Reporting

An organisation that applies GRI 1 may make one of two claims:

- **Reported with reference to the GRI Standards** — selected disclosures used; a GRI content index is required.
- **Reported in accordance with the GRI Standards** — fully applies GRI 1, uses GRI 2 and GRI 3, applies any applicable Sector Standards, and reports on each material topic using the Topic Standards.

Omissions are allowed under specified reasons (confidentiality constraints, legal prohibitions, information unavailable or incomplete) and must be disclosed in the content index.

## Interoperability

GRI and the ISSB signed a Memorandum of Understanding in 2022 that coordinates work on the two materiality lenses — impact (GRI) and financial (ISSB) — and seeks to let an organisation produce one dataset that can be reported under either regime where appropriate. GRI has also published:

- **ESRS-GRI interoperability index**, co-signed with EFRAG, mapping every ESRS datapoint to the GRI disclosure it corresponds to. In practice, a company reporting under ESRS satisfies most GRI "in accordance with" disclosure obligations, provided it addresses the GRI-specific stakeholder-engagement and management-approach requirements explicitly.
- Alignment with the [[OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises]], the [[UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights]], and the [[UN Global Compact]].

## Human Rights and Social Topics

The 2021 revision reinforced GRI's status as the most comprehensive social-reporting framework. GRI 2 asks for policies to respect human rights, due diligence processes aligned with the UNGPs and OECD Guidelines, and operation of grievance mechanisms; GRI 3 requires explicit identification of human-rights impacts; and the 400-series Topic Standards (401–418) cover employment, labour relations, occupational health and safety, training, diversity and equal opportunity, non-discrimination, freedom of association and collective bargaining, child labour, forced labour, security practices, rights of indigenous peoples, local-community impacts, supplier social assessment, public policy and customer privacy.

## Governance and Due-Process

GRI's standard-setting is independent, under the Global Sustainability Standards Board (GSSB). Each standard undergoes multi-stakeholder consultation, public exposure, and GSSB approval. The due-process model is a direct parallel of IASB / ISSB procedure and is one reason regulators treat GRI output as regulator-grade input.

## Implications for SMEs

GRI is designed around any size of organisation, but the "in accordance with" level is heavy for a small company. SMEs typically either:

- apply GRI *with reference to* for the Topic Standards most relevant to their stakeholders (a lean report built on GRI 1 and a handful of Topic Standards), or
- report under [[Voluntary Sustainability Reporting Standard for SMEs (VSME)]] and use the ESRS-GRI interoperability index to map upward when a specific stakeholder — bank, investor, large buyer — asks for a GRI-formatted view.
