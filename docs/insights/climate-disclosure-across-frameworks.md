# Climate Disclosure Across Frameworks

Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) operating in or supplying to the EU face a converging set of climate disclosure obligations and expectations drawn from at least four distinct regimes: the [[European Sustainability Reporting Standards]] (ESRS), the Task Force on Climate-Related Financial Disclosures (TCFD), the Greenhouse Gas (GHG) Protocol, and the EU Taxonomy Regulation. Although each framework has its own legal basis, scope, and methodology, they share significant conceptual territory — particularly around emissions measurement, climate risk, and the definition of sustainable activities — and understanding where they overlap can help an SME avoid duplicating effort while satisfying multiple audiences.

## ESRS and the SME Entry Point

The ESRS were published in 2023 and became mandatory for large and listed companies under the [[Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive]] (CSRD) from January 2024 [[esrs]]. SMEs are not directly obligated under the CSRD, but they face indirect pressure from two directions: large CSRD-obligated customers that need supply-chain data, and investors or lenders influenced by the [[EU Taxonomy Regulation]] and the Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation (SFDR) [[eu-taxonomy]].

The proportionate on-ramp for SMEs is the [[Voluntary Sustainability Reporting Standard for SMEs]] (VSME), which maps its basic and extended module chapters directly to ESRS topic areas [[esrs]]. Specifically, ESRS chapter E1 (climate change and energy) corresponds to VSME chapters B2, C2, B3, C3, and C4 [[esrs]]. This structural alignment means that an SME completing the relevant VSME chapters produces information that is immediately legible to a large CSRD-obligated undertaking in its value chain [[esrs]].

A distinctive requirement of ESRS — and by extension the VSME — is the [[Double Materiality Assessment]] (DMA), which asks organisations to evaluate both [[financial materiality]] (how sustainability factors affect the enterprise's own financial position) and [[impact materiality]] (how the enterprise affects people and the environment) [[esrs]]. This dual lens is not present in TCFD, which focuses solely on financial materiality, making the ESRS approach broader in scope by design [[esrs]] [[tcfd]].

## TCFD: The Climate-Risk Architecture

The TCFD, launched in 2017, structures climate disclosure around four pillars: Governance, Strategy, Risk Management, and Metrics and Targets [[tcfd]]. It was designed to produce decision-useful information for investors and lenders assessing climate-related financial risks, and it subsequently served as the foundational basis for the standards developed by the International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB) [[tcfd]]. The Carbon Disclosure Project (CDP) methodology also draws on TCFD recommendations [[tcfd]].

For an SME, TCFD is not legally mandated within the EU, but its four-pillar structure provides a useful internal organising logic that maps well onto what ESRS E1 and the VSME climate chapters require. Specifically:

- **Governance** (TCFD) ↔ ESRS governance disclosures and VSME governance chapters
- **Strategy** (TCFD) ↔ ESRS impact and risk disclosures, including transition and physical risk
- **Risk Management** (TCFD) ↔ ESRS [[Double Materiality Assessment]]
- **Metrics and Targets** (TCFD) ↔ GHG emissions KPIs and targets under VSME and ESRS E1

Because the ISSB built on TCFD foundations, SMEs that structure their internal climate analysis around TCFD pillars are simultaneously preparing content that feeds into ESRS-compatible reporting and into any ISSB-aligned requests from international customers or investors [[tcfd]].

## GHG Protocol: The Measurement Layer

Regardless of which disclosure framework an SME reports under, the underlying emissions data must be measured consistently. The GHG Protocol — referenced across ESRS, VSME, and TCFD-aligned disclosures — categorises emissions into three scopes [[ghg-protocol]]:

- **Scope 1**: Direct emissions from sources owned or controlled by the organisation
- **Scope 2**: Indirect emissions from purchased energy
- **Scope 3**: All other indirect emissions across the value chain

The VSME framework incorporates Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions measurement in alignment with the ESRS E1 climate theme [[ghg-protocol]]. Scope 3 emissions are particularly significant for SMEs embedded in supply chains, because their upstream and downstream emissions contribute to the reported impacts of larger CSRD-obligated entities [[ghg-protocol]]. A large customer's ESRS Scope 3 disclosure is only as accurate as the emissions data it receives from suppliers — meaning an SME's GHG reporting is not merely an internal matter but a data-quality issue for the entire supply chain [[ghg-protocol]].

The Bordvol case study illustrates how an SME can operationalise this: Bordvol sourced CO₂ data via a logistics service provider, gathered energy consumption data per production site, and incorporated total Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions as key performance indicators (KPIs) in its sustainability dashboard, with a target of [[climate neutrality]] by 2035 [[ghg-protocol]].

## EU Taxonomy: The Activity-Classification Layer

The [[EU Taxonomy Regulation]] sits alongside but distinct from ESRS. Rather than requiring narrative or quantitative climate disclosures, it establishes a classification system for determining whether specific economic activities qualify as environmentally sustainable [[eu-taxonomy]]. It operates within the EU's broader sustainable finance architecture alongside the SFDR, which requires financial market participants to disclose how sustainability risks are integrated into investment decisions [[eu-taxonomy]].

For SMEs, the Taxonomy is relevant in two ways. First, if an SME's activities are financed by investment products subject to SFDR, the fund manager may ask whether those activities are Taxonomy-aligned. Second, the EU Taxonomy is listed among the primary reference frameworks that businesses — including SMEs — may need to consider when structuring their ESG strategies and reporting [[eu-taxonomy]].

An SME's GHG emissions data (measured under GHG Protocol categories and disclosed via VSME/ESRS channels) feeds directly into Taxonomy alignment assessments, because many Taxonomy criteria include greenhouse gas emission thresholds or targets as Do No Significant Harm (DNSH) conditions.

## A Practical Disclosure Pathway for an SME

Drawing the frameworks together, an SME can approach climate disclosure in four sequential steps without duplicating effort:

1. **Measure emissions first.** Using GHG Protocol Scope 1, 2, and 3 categories as the common measurement layer ensures that the resulting data satisfies the quantitative requirements of VSME, ESRS E1, and TCFD's Metrics and Targets pillar simultaneously [[ghg-protocol]] [[esrs]] [[tcfd]].

2. **Conduct a proportionate materiality assessment.** Even without a full ESRS DMA, assessing which climate risks are financially material (TCFD logic) and which of the SME's activities have environmental impact (ESRS impact materiality logic) positions the organisation for both frameworks [[esrs]] [[tcfd]].

3. **Report via the VSME.** The VSME's chapter structure maps directly to ESRS E1, meaning disclosure under VSME chapters B2, C2, B3, C3, and C4 simultaneously satisfies the information needs of CSRD-obligated value-chain partners [[esrs]].

4. **Check Taxonomy alignment.** Where the SME's activities fall within Taxonomy-covered sectors, verify whether GHG-related Do No Significant Harm conditions are met, since this information is increasingly requested by financial institutions subject to SFDR [[eu-taxonomy]].

The TCFD four-pillar structure (Governance, Strategy, Risk Management, Metrics and Targets) can serve as the internal architecture that organises all of the above, given that it underpins both the ISSB standards and the CDP methodology — two channels through which international customers and investors may separately request climate information [[tcfd]].
