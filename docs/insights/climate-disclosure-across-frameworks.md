# Climate Disclosure Across Frameworks

An SME operating in or supplying into the EU market faces climate disclosure obligations and expectations from multiple directions simultaneously: mandatory EU standards, international investor-facing frameworks, greenhouse gas (GHG) accounting protocols, and sustainable-finance classification rules. These regimes overlap substantially on climate topics—particularly GHG emissions measurement and climate-related risk—yet differ in their legal basis, materiality logic, and intended audience. Understanding where they converge and diverge allows an SME to build a single, coherent disclosure effort that satisfies multiple frameworks at once.

## 1. The EU Regulatory Layer: ESRS and the VSME

The [[Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive]] (CSRD) requires large EU undertakings to report under the [[European Sustainability Reporting Standards]] (ESRS) from January 2024 [[esrs]]. SMEs are not directly subject to the CSRD, but they are affected indirectly: large CSRD-obligated entities in their supply chains request climate and sustainability data to complete their own disclosures [[esrs]].

To address this, the [[Voluntary Sustainability Reporting Standard for SMEs]] (VSME) was developed as a proportionate counterpart to the ESRS [[esrs]]. Its chapters are mapped directly to ESRS topic areas: for instance, ESRS chapter E1 on climate change and energy corresponds to VSME chapters B2, C2, B3, C3, and C4 [[esrs]]. This structural alignment ensures that an SME reporting under the VSME produces information that is intelligible and directly usable by large CSRD-obligated undertakings [[esrs]].

### Double Materiality

A defining feature of the ESRS is the requirement for a [[Double Materiality Assessment]] (DMA). Under [[double materiality]], companies must disclose both [[financial materiality]]—the effect of sustainability factors on the organisation's financial position—and [[impact materiality]]—the organisation's effects on people and the environment [[esrs]]. The VSME applies a proportionate version of this logic, allowing SMEs to assess and report on both dimensions without the full DMA burden imposed on large companies.

## 2. The International Investor Layer: TCFD

The [[Task Force on Climate-Related Financial Disclosures]] (TCFD) was launched in 2017 to help organisations disclose climate-related financial risks in a consistent and decision-useful manner [[tcfd]]. Its framework is structured around four core pillars:

1. **Governance** – Board and management oversight of climate-related risks and opportunities
2. **Strategy** – Actual and potential climate impacts on business strategy and financial planning
3. **Risk Management** – Processes for identifying, assessing, and managing climate-related risks
4. **Metrics and Targets** – Metrics used to assess climate risks and opportunities, including GHG emissions

TCFD disclosures are mandatory for listed companies in several jurisdictions, including the United Kingdom, Japan, and New Zealand [[tcfd]]. For SMEs, TCFD is typically voluntary but increasingly expected by investors, banks, and large corporate customers operating in those jurisdictions.

### TCFD and ESRS: Where They Overlap

TCFD subsequently served as the foundational basis for the standards developed by the International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB) [[tcfd]], and its recommendations also underpin the methodology used by the Carbon Disclosure Project (CDP) [[tcfd]]. The ESRS draws on similar pillars—governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics—meaning that an SME preparing a VSME-aligned climate disclosure will have addressed the substantive content that TCFD-aligned investors are looking for. The key difference is materiality logic: ESRS/VSME applies [[double materiality]], whereas TCFD focuses on single, investor-facing [[financial materiality]] and [[climate-related financial risk]].

## 3. The Emissions Accounting Layer: GHG Protocol and Scope Reporting

Regardless of which disclosure framework an SME uses, quantifying GHG emissions requires a consistent accounting methodology. The GHG Protocol is the international standard underpinning Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions classifications [[ghg-protocol]].

- **Scope 1:** Direct emissions from owned or controlled sources
- **Scope 2:** Indirect emissions from purchased energy
- **Scope 3:** All other indirect emissions across the value chain

The VSME framework incorporates all three scopes [[ghg-protocol]], reflecting its alignment with ESRS topic E1 on climate change and energy [[esrs]]. [[Scope 3 emissions]] are particularly relevant for SMEs embedded in supply chains, as their upstream and downstream value-chain partners generate emissions data that contribute to the reported impacts of larger CSRD-obligated entities [[ghg-protocol]].

### Practical Illustration: Bordvol

The Bordvol case study demonstrates how an SME can operationalise this layer. Bordvol built a sustainability dashboard incorporating total CO₂ emissions across Scopes 1, 2, and 3 as measurable [[key performance indicators]] (KPIs), with CO₂ data sourced via a logistics service provider and energy consumption data gathered per production site [[ghg-protocol]]. The organisation set a target of [[climate neutrality]] by 2035 [[ghg-protocol]]. This approach simultaneously satisfies the GHG accounting requirements embedded in the VSME, provides the Scope-level data that TCFD's "Metrics and Targets" pillar requires, and generates the emissions evidence base needed for EU Taxonomy assessments.

## 4. The Sustainable Finance Layer: EU Taxonomy

The EU Taxonomy Regulation establishes a classification system for environmentally sustainable economic activities and forms a key component of the EU sustainable finance architecture [[eu-taxonomy]]. It is discussed alongside the Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation (SFDR), which requires financial market participants to disclose how sustainability risks are integrated into investment decisions [[eu-taxonomy]]. Together, these instruments direct capital toward sustainable outcomes [[eu-taxonomy]].

For an SME, the EU Taxonomy is relevant in two ways. First, it is listed among the primary reference frameworks that businesses, including SMEs, may need to consider when structuring their ESG strategies and reporting [[eu-taxonomy]]. Second, if the SME seeks green financing or investment from funds subject to SFDR, the fund manager will need to assess whether the SME's activities qualify as taxonomy-aligned—making the SME's own climate disclosures material to the financing relationship.

The EU Taxonomy does not itself prescribe how emissions are measured; it relies on the emissions accounting layer (Scope 1/2/3 via GHG Protocol) and the ESRS/VSME disclosure layer for underlying data.

## 6. Where the Frameworks Reinforce Each Other

Several structural overlaps reduce the disclosure burden for an SME that addresses them in an integrated way:

- **TCFD pillars mirror ESRS structure.** Because TCFD served as a foundational basis for ISSB standards [[tcfd]], and the ESRS was developed in dialogue with those standards, the four TCFD pillars (Governance, Strategy, Risk Management, Metrics and Targets) are substantially covered by ESRS/VSME climate chapters. A VSME-compliant climate disclosure therefore provides much of the content TCFD-aligned stakeholders expect.

- **GHG Protocol Scope data serves multiple frameworks at once.** Scope 1, 2, and 3 data collected for VSME reporting [[ghg-protocol]] simultaneously satisfies the Metrics and Targets pillar of TCFD and provides the emissions evidence base for EU Taxonomy assessments [[eu-taxonomy]].

- **VSME-to-ESRS structural alignment reduces supply-chain friction.** Because VSME chapters are mapped directly to ESRS topic areas [[esrs]], an SME's VSME climate disclosure can be passed directly to large CSRD-obligated customers without translation, reducing the administrative burden on both sides.

- **EU Taxonomy and SFDR create a financing incentive to disclose.** The EU Taxonomy's role in the sustainable finance architecture [[eu-taxonomy]] means that SMEs seeking green loans or sustainable investment have a commercial incentive to produce taxonomy-relevant disclosures, which in turn reinforces the emissions and climate-risk data already collected for VSME/TCFD purposes.

## 7. Key Differences to Manage

Despite their overlaps, the frameworks retain important differences that an SME must navigate:

- **Materiality logic diverges.** ESRS/VSME requires [[double materiality]]—both the firm's effect on the environment and the environment's effect on the firm [[esrs]]. TCFD focuses solely on [[financial materiality]] and [[climate-related financial risk]] for investors [[tcfd]]. An SME must decide whether to prepare a single double-materiality assessment (satisfying ESRS/VSME) or a supplementary investor-facing single-materiality narrative (satisfying TCFD expectations).

- **Legal obligation differs by jurisdiction and size.** ESRS is mandatory for large EU entities under CSRD; VSME is voluntary for SMEs [[esrs]]. TCFD is mandatory for listed companies in the UK, Japan, and New Zealand [[tcfd]] but voluntary elsewhere. SMEs must identify which obligations are legally binding for their specific situation.

- **EU Taxonomy requires activity-level evidence.** Unlike ESRS/VSME or TCFD, which focus on entity-level disclosure, the EU Taxonomy operates at the level of individual economic activities and applies technical screening criteria [[eu-taxonomy]]. This requires more granular operational data than standard climate reporting.
